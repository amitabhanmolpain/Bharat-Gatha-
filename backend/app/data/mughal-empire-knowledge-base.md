## Agra Fort — Overview

Agra Fort, also known as the Red Fort of Agra, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site located on the banks of the Yamuna River in Agra, Uttar Pradesh. It was the main residence of the Mughal emperors until 1638, when the capital shifted to Delhi. The fort was originally a brick fort held by the Chauhan Rajputs, but Emperor Akbar rebuilt it substantially starting in 1565, using red sandstone. Later emperors, especially Shah Jahan, added white marble structures inside it. The fort's walls are about 2.5 kilometers in circumference and up to 20 meters high.

## Agra Fort — Amar Singh Gate

Amar Singh Gate is the main public entrance to Agra Fort today. It is named after Amar Singh Rathore, a 17th-century Rajput noble in Shah Jahan's court known for a dramatic act of defiance against a court official, after which he leapt from the fort walls on horseback to escape — his horse died from the fall but he escaped (some versions of the legend say he did not survive; historical accounts vary). The gate is built in a zigzag, double-walled design specifically so that war elephants could not gain a straight run-up to ram the doors, and so attackers would be exposed to defensive fire from multiple angles as they turned.

## Agra Fort — Diwan-i-Aam (Hall of Public Audience)

The Diwan-i-Aam was where the emperor held public court, heard petitions from ordinary citizens, and administered justice. It is a large hall with a raised marble throne platform where the emperor would sit, flanked by officials. Built under Shah Jahan around 1628, it reflects Mughal practices of accessible governance, where any subject could theoretically present a grievance directly to the emperor.

## Agra Fort — Anguri Bagh

Anguri Bagh ("Grape Garden") is a formal Persian-style garden within Agra Fort, laid out in a geometric grid pattern (charbagh style — a four-quadrant garden design symbolizing the four rivers of paradise in Islamic tradition). Despite its name, historians debate whether grapes were actually cultivated here; it may have simply followed the naming convention of Persian pleasure gardens.

## Agra Fort — Sheesh Mahal (Palace of Mirrors)

The Sheesh Mahal is a small chamber within the female quarters of Agra Fort, decorated with thousands of tiny convex mirror fragments (glass mirror-work, known as "shisheh") set into the plaster ceiling and walls. The design meant that even a single candle's flame would be reflected many times over, creating the effect of a starlit sky. This mirror-inlay technique was a hallmark of high Mughal decorative art, also seen in the Sheesh Mahal of Amber Fort in Jaipur.

## Agra Fort — Khas Mahal

Khas Mahal was Shah Jahan's private palace within Agra Fort, built primarily in white marble, overlooking the Yamuna River — offering a direct view toward the site where the Taj Mahal would later be built. It was used as private living quarters for the emperor and his family, distinct from the more public ceremonial halls.

## Agra Fort — Musamman Burj (Shah Burj)

Musamman Burj is an octagonal tower within Agra Fort, built by Shah Jahan, from which — according to widely repeated historical accounts — he was imprisoned by his son Aurangzeb from 1658 until his death in 1666, after Aurangzeb seized the throne in a war of succession. From this tower, Shah Jahan reportedly could see the Taj Mahal, the mausoleum he built for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, in the distance. This imprisonment followed a succession war among Shah Jahan's sons after he fell seriously ill in 1657.

## Taj Mahal — Overview

The Taj Mahal is a white marble mausoleum commissioned by Emperor Shah Jahan in 1631 in memory of his wife, Mumtaz Mahal, who died during childbirth. Construction took approximately 22 years (completed around 1653) and involved thousands of artisans and craftsmen from across the Mughal Empire and beyond, including specialists from Central Asia and Persia. It is widely regarded as the finest example of Mughal architecture, blending Persian, Islamic, and Indian architectural styles. It was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.

## Taj Mahal — Darwaza-i-Rauza (The Great Gate)

The Great Gate is the main entrance to the Taj Mahal complex, built from red sandstone with white marble inlay. It is inscribed with verses from the Quran, written in a calligraphic style using black marble inlay (a technique called "pietra dura" combined with calligraphy). One well-known inscription is a verse addressing the soul, inviting it to return to God "at peace" (or "well pleased") — reflecting the mausoleum's function as a spiritual resting place.

## Taj Mahal — Charbagh Gardens

The gardens leading up to the Taj Mahal follow the Persian "charbagh" (four-garden) layout — a square garden divided into four equal quarters by walkways or flowing water, symbolizing the four rivers of Paradise described in the Quran. A central raised marble water tank (sometimes called the "reflecting pool") sits at the intersection, designed so the Taj Mahal's reflection appears in the still water.

## Taj Mahal — Main Mausoleum

Inside the main dome sits an octagonal chamber containing cenotaphs (memorial tombs) of Mumtaz Mahal and, added later, Shah Jahan himself. These visible cenotaphs are decorative and do not contain the actual remains. The real graves lie in a plain, unadorned crypt directly below, at garden level — this follows Islamic tradition, which generally discourages ostentatious display over actual burial sites. The dome above the chamber rises about 73 meters and is flanked by four minarets, each built slightly tilted outward from vertical, partly so that in the event of an earthquake they would fall away from the main tomb rather than onto it.

## Taj Mahal — Mahtab Bagh and the "Black Taj Mahal" Legend

Mahtab Bagh ("Moonlight Garden") is a garden complex on the opposite bank of the Yamuna River from the Taj Mahal. A popular legend claims Shah Jahan intended to build a mirror-image mausoleum for himself here, constructed in black marble, connected to the white Taj Mahal by a bridge — so the two tombs would reflect one another across the river. According to the legend, this plan was abandoned after Aurangzeb deposed and imprisoned Shah Jahan in 1658.

Archaeological excavations at Mahtab Bagh in the 1990s (led by archaeologist Elizabeth Moynihan) uncovered discolored, blackened stone in the garden's foundations, which is part of what fuels the legend. However, most historians and archaeologists now believe these are simply weathered white marble or sandstone elements that darkened over centuries of exposure, and that Mahtab Bagh was intended purely as an ornamental garden for viewing the Taj Mahal by moonlight, not the foundation of a second mausoleum. There is no definitive documentary evidence (no Mughal-era text or architectural plan) confirming a "Black Taj" was ever formally planned. The legend remains unproven but persistent in popular imagination.

## Red Fort (Delhi) — Overview

The Red Fort (Lal Qila) in Delhi was built by Shah Jahan starting in 1638, when he moved the Mughal capital from Agra to the newly founded city of Shahjahanabad (now part of Old Delhi). It served as the main residence of Mughal emperors for nearly 200 years, until 1857. It is constructed primarily of red sandstone, giving it its name, and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Since India's independence in 1947, the Prime Minister has hoisted the national flag and delivered an address from the Red Fort's ramparts every Independence Day (August 15).

## Red Fort — Lahori Gate

Lahori Gate is the main public entrance to the Red Fort, facing toward the city of Lahore (hence the name). It is the gate from which the Prime Minister addresses the nation each Independence Day.

## Red Fort — Chhatta Chowk

Chhatta Chowk is a covered market street just inside the Red Fort's Lahori Gate, built during Shah Jahan's reign. It was historically a bazaar reserved for the royal household and nobility, where merchants sold luxury goods — silk, jewelry, and other fine items — to the Mughal court. Foreign travelers of the period wrote about the wealth of goods on display here.

## Red Fort — Diwan-i-Khas (Hall of Private Audience)

The Diwan-i-Khas was where the emperor met with nobles, foreign dignitaries, and high officials in a more private setting than the Diwan-i-Aam. It once housed the legendary Peacock Throne (Takht-e-Tavus), an elaborately jeweled golden throne commissioned by Shah Jahan, said to be encrusted with precious stones including the Koh-i-Noor diamond in some historical accounts. In 1739, the Persian ruler Nadir Shah invaded Delhi, defeated the Mughal army, and looted the city, taking the Peacock Throne back to Persia. Its ultimate fate afterward is not fully documented — it was reportedly broken up or lost over subsequent decades of Persian political turmoil.

## Red Fort — Rang Mahal

Rang Mahal ("Palace of Colors") was part of the emperor's private quarters, historically used by the royal women of the household. It featured a marble water channel called the "Nahr-i-Behisht" ("Stream of Paradise") running through the floor, part of the fort's larger cooling water system that channeled water from the Yamuna River through several palace buildings.

## Red Fort — Aurangzeb's Seizure of Power

After Shah Jahan fell seriously ill in 1657, a war of succession broke out among his four sons. Aurangzeb ultimately defeated his brothers (including the eldest, Dara Shikoh, who was executed), imprisoned his father Shah Jahan in Agra Fort, and crowned himself emperor in 1658. Aurangzeb ruled until 1707 and expanded the Mughal Empire to its greatest territorial extent, though his reign is also associated with prolonged wars and rising internal unrest that contributed to the empire's later decline.
