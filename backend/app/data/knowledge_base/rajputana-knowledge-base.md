## Chittorgarh Fort — Overview

Chittorgarh Fort is one of the largest forts in India, located in Chittorgarh, Rajasthan, and was the capital of the Mewar kingdom under the Sisodia Rajput dynasty for several centuries. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (part of the "Hill Forts of Rajasthan" designation). The fort complex covers roughly 700 acres atop a hill and includes palaces, temples, and towers built across different periods of Mewar rule. It was besieged multiple times in its history, notably by Alauddin Khilji in 1303 and by Akbar in 1567–68.

## Chittorgarh Fort — Vijay Stambh (Tower of Victory)

The Vijay Stambh is a nine-storey tower, approximately 37 meters (122 feet) tall, built by Rana Kumbha between 1458 and 1468 to commemorate his military victory over the combined armies of Malwa and Gujarat in 1440. The tower is covered in intricate carvings depicting Hindu deities, scenes from mythology, and figures from the Mewar court. An internal staircase of 157 steps leads to the top, which offers a panoramic view of the fort and surrounding countryside. An inscription on the tower records Rana Kumbha's lineage and achievements.

## Chittorgarh Fort — Rana Kumbha

Rana Kumbha ruled Mewar from 1433 to 1468. He is credited with major fort-building and temple-building activity across Mewar, including work at both Chittorgarh and Kumbhalgarh. He was also a patron of the arts and reportedly authored several works on music and literature.

## Chittorgarh Fort — Jauhar Kund and the 1303 Siege

In 1303, Alauddin Khilji, the Sultan of Delhi, laid siege to Chittorgarh. After the fort's defenses were overwhelmed, historical and traditional accounts state that the Rajput women of the fort, led by the queen, performed jauhar — a mass act of self-immolation — to avoid capture and preserve their honor according to the customs of the time, while the Rajput men rode out to fight to the death. This event is commemorated at Jauhar Kund within the fort. Popular legend closely associates this 1303 siege with Rani Padmini, though the historical accuracy of the Padmini legend itself (as opposed to the siege and jauhar, which are documented) is debated among historians, since the earliest surviving version of her story appears in a 16th-century poem written over 200 years after the events, rather than in contemporary records.

## Chittorgarh Fort — Panna Dhai

Panna Dhai was a royal nurse (wet-nurse) in the Mewar court in the 1530s. According to widely told legend, when an usurper, Banbir, sought to kill the young heir Udai Singh II (who later founded Udaipur) to seize the throne, Panna Dhai substituted her own son in the prince's bed to save him, and her son was killed in his place. She then smuggled the real prince out of the fort to safety. This story is celebrated in Rajasthani folklore as an example of extraordinary loyalty and sacrifice.

## Chittorgarh Fort — Meera Bai

Meera Bai was a 16th-century mystic poet-saint associated with Chittorgarh, where she is said to have lived after marrying into the Mewar royal family. She composed devotional songs (bhajans) dedicated to Lord Krishna, many of which are still sung across India today. A temple dedicated to her exists within the Chittorgarh Fort complex. Her poetry is known for its themes of intense personal devotion (bhakti) and defiance of social conventions of her time.

## Kumbhalgarh Fort — Overview

Kumbhalgarh Fort was built by Rana Kumbha in the 15th century (mid-1400s) and served as an important Mewar stronghold and refuge. It is famous for its massive perimeter wall, often cited as the second-longest continuous wall in the world after the Great Wall of China, stretching approximately 36 kilometers, wide enough in places for several horses to ride abreast. It is also a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The fort's design includes seven fortified gates and multiple watchtowers.

## Kumbhalgarh Fort — Defensive Design

The fort's gates are arranged in a winding, zigzag pattern along the approach road, forcing attackers to make repeated turns and slow their advance, exposing them to defenders positioned on the walls above. This defensive architecture reflects broader Rajput fort-building practices seen across the region.

## Kumbhalgarh Fort — Maharana Pratap

Maharana Pratap, one of the most celebrated Rajput rulers, was born at Kumbhalgarh in 1540. He ruled Mewar from 1572 and is remembered for his prolonged resistance against Mughal expansion under Akbar, most famously at the Battle of Haldighati in 1576. He is also remembered for his devoted warhorse, Chetak, who reportedly carried him to safety after being wounded in that battle before dying from its injuries. Pratap refused to submit to Mughal authority throughout his reign, unlike several other Rajput rulers of the period who formed alliances with the Mughal court.

## Kumbhalgarh Fort — Badal Mahal

Badal Mahal ("Cloud Palace") is a palace structure within Kumbhalgarh Fort, notable for wall paintings and frescoes in the Mewar painting style — a regional school of miniature painting known for vivid natural pigments (often derived from minerals, plants, and other organic sources) and detailed depictions of court life, mythology, and nature.

## Jaigarh Fort — Overview

Jaigarh Fort overlooks the city of Amber near Jaipur and was built under the Kachwaha Rajput dynasty, associated with Sawai Jai Singh II (who also founded the city of Jaipur in 1727). It was primarily a military fort, used for manufacturing weapons and artillery, and connected to the neighboring Amber Fort by fortified passages.

## Jaigarh Fort — Jaivana Cannon

The Jaivana Cannon, cast in 1720, is often cited as the largest cannon on wheels ever built, with a barrel around 20 feet (6 meters) long. It was reportedly test-fired only once; accounts suggest the projectile traveled a very long distance, though exact figures vary between sources. It remains on display at Jaigarh Fort today.

## Jal Mahal — Overview

Jal Mahal ("Water Palace") sits in the middle of Man Sagar Lake in Jaipur, associated with the Kachwaha dynasty of Amber/Jaipur (the same ruling family connected to Jaigarh Fort). It was built and later renovated in the 18th century, notably under Sawai Jai Singh II. The palace is designed so that four of its five floors are usually submerged underwater when the lake is full, with only the top floor and its rooftop pavilions visible above the surface — an example of Rajput water engineering and architectural planning around the lake's water levels.

## Kuldhara — Overview and the Real History Behind the Legend

Kuldhara is an abandoned village near Jaisalmer, historically inhabited by Paliwal Brahmins, a prosperous merchant and agricultural community. According to local history and oral tradition, in the early 19th century (commonly dated to around 1825), the residents of Kuldhara and a number of surrounding Paliwal villages (accounts vary, often citing around 83 villages) abandoned their homes overnight and left the region entirely.

The traditional and most historically supported explanation centers on the actions of Salim Singh, a powerful and reportedly tyrannical prime minister (diwan) serving under the rulers of Jaisalmer at the time. Two grievances are commonly cited:

1. **Forced marriage demand**: Salim Singh is said to have demanded marriage to the daughter of a village chief (or a similarly prominent local figure), threatening the village with severe retaliation if refused.
2. **Oppressive taxation**: Salim Singh is also described as having imposed increasingly harsh and arbitrary taxes on the wealthy Paliwal community, making their continued life in the region economically unsustainable.

Facing these pressures, the Paliwal communities are said to have made a collective decision to leave overnight rather than submit, reportedly also vowing that the villages would never be resettled — which locals point to as the origin of the village's continued abandonment centuries later. While a popular "curse" narrative has developed around this vow in modern retellings (often framed as a supernatural curse causing misfortune to anyone who resettles there), the underlying historical account centers on real grievances — abuse of power and unjust taxation — rather than the supernatural framing that tourism folklore has since attached to the story. Kuldhara today is a protected, ASI-maintained site open to visitors.

## Bhangarh Fort — Overview and Local Legend

Bhangarh Fort, located in the Alwar district of Rajasthan, was built in the late 16th century by Bhagwant Das and later expanded by his son Madho Singh I, who were connected to the Kachwaha (Amber) ruling family. The town surrounding the fort was reportedly a thriving settlement before it was abandoned; the exact reasons for its abandonment are not conclusively documented, though historians commonly point to factors such as famine, drought, or the broader decline of regional administration rather than any single supernatural event.

Bhangarh is widely known in popular culture as one of India's most famous "haunted" locations. The Archaeological Survey of India has posted signage at the site restricting visitor entry between sunset and sunrise. A popular local legend attributes the town's ruin to a curse: in one common version, a tantrik (mystic/sorcerer) named Singhia fell in love with the local princess and attempted to use black magic to enchant her, but the plan was discovered and thwarted, and before his execution he cursed the town, dooming it to ruin. This story exists in several varying versions across local oral tradition, and no contemporary historical documentation confirms the tantrik legend as fact — it is best treated as folklore layered onto a site whose actual abandonment likely had more mundane causes.

## Jaisalmer — Overview

Jaisalmer, founded in 1156 by Rawal Jaisal, is ruled historically by the Bhati Rajput dynasty — a separate lineage from both the Sisodias of Mewar and the Kachwahas of Amber/Jaipur. Jaisalmer Fort, sometimes called the "Golden Fort" for the honey-colored sandstone it's built from, is one of the very few "living forts" in the world, meaning a significant residential population still lives and runs businesses inside its walls today, unlike most historical forts which are purely monuments. The city historically prospered as a trading post along caravan routes connecting India to Central Asia and the Middle East, particularly for the silk and spice trade.

## Jaisalmer — Traditional Games and Crafts

**Chaupar** (sometimes spelled Chausar) is a traditional cross-shaped board game played with cowrie shells or dice, widely regarded as an ancestor of the modern game Ludo. It was historically played across Rajput and Mughal royal courts, including a large marble Chaupar court said to have existed in some palace complexes for life-sized versions of the game played with courtiers as pieces.

**Kathputli** is a traditional Rajasthani string-puppet art form, believed to have originated in Rajasthan and traditionally performed by itinerant puppeteer communities (notably the Bhat community) who used the puppet shows to narrate folk tales, historical stories, and moral lessons to rural audiences. The puppets are typically carved from wood, dressed in colorful traditional attire, and manipulated using strings by a puppeteer who often also narrates or sings the accompanying story.

**Ganjifa** is a traditional card game using circular, hand-painted playing cards, believed to have been introduced to India via Persian and Mughal courtly culture and later adapted into various regional Indian styles. Ganjifa sets often depict mythological or courtly themes and were historically associated with royal and noble leisure activities.
