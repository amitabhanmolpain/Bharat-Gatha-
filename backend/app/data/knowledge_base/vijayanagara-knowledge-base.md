## Vijayanagara Empire — Overview and Founding

The Vijayanagara Empire was founded in 1336 CE by two brothers, Harihara I and Bukka Raya I, in what is now Karnataka. According to tradition, they were guided and inspired by the sage and scholar Vidyaranya (also known as Madhava), who is credited with encouraging the founding of the empire as a bulwark against invasions from the north. The empire took its name from its capital city, Vijayanagara ("City of Victory"), located on the banks of the Tungabhadra River, at the site now known as Hampi. At its peak in the 15th and 16th centuries, the empire controlled much of South India and was renowned by foreign travelers for its extraordinary wealth. The empire ultimately declined after its defeat at the Battle of Talikota in 1565, after which its capital was sacked and largely abandoned.

## Vijayanagara Empire — Sage Vidyaranya

Vidyaranya was a 14th-century scholar and the head (jagadguru) of the Sringeri Sharada Peetham, a major monastic institution. He is traditionally credited as the spiritual advisor and guiding figure behind the founding of the Vijayanagara Empire, providing both ideological legitimacy and strategic guidance to Harihara I and Bukka Raya I in their early campaigns. The Vijayanagara rulers continued to patronize the Sringeri monastery throughout much of the empire's history, reflecting Vidyaranya's foundational role.

## Anegundi — Overview

Anegundi is a town located across the Tungabhadra River from Hampi, and is considered older than Hampi itself — it served as an early administrative center before the Vijayanagara capital was formally established at Hampi. Anegundi is also traditionally associated with Kishkindha, the mythological monkey-kingdom described in the Hindu epic the Ramayana, said to be the domain of the monkey-king Sugriva and the birthplace of Hanuman. This dual identity — a real historical settlement layered with deep mythological association — makes Anegundi a site where legend and documented history intersect closely.

## Hampi — Overview

Hampi, the ruins of the former Vijayanagara capital, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site located in present-day Karnataka. At its height, foreign travelers — including the Portuguese chronicler Domingo Paes, who visited around 1520 — described Vijayanagara as one of the largest and richest cities in the world at the time, comparing it favorably to Rome in scale. Accounts from this period describe extraordinary wealth on public display, including reports that diamonds and other precious stones were sold openly in the city's marketplaces.

## Hampi — Virupaksha Temple

Virupaksha Temple, dedicated to a form of the god Shiva (Virupaksha) and his consort Pampa, is one of the oldest structures in Hampi and remains an active place of worship today, predating the Vijayanagara Empire itself. The temple's name is closely tied to the origin of the name "Hampi," which derives from "Pampa" — the local river goddess associated with the site in Hindu tradition. Within the temple complex, a small dark chamber contains a naturally occurring optical phenomenon: light entering through a small opening projects an inverted image of the temple's main tower (gopuram) onto an interior wall, functioning similarly to a camera obscura — a striking example of architectural or accidental optical design predating modern photographic technology.

## Hampi — Hampi Bazaar

The main bazaar street facing Virupaksha Temple was historically one of Vijayanagara's principal marketplaces. Foreign travelers' accounts from the 16th century describe it as a bustling trade hub where goods including gems, textiles, and spices were sold, reflecting the city's position as a major center of commerce during the empire's peak.

## Hampi — Vittala Temple

Vittala Temple, built primarily during the reign of Krishnadevaraya (ruled 1509–1529), is considered the most architecturally elaborate temple complex in Hampi. It features an iconic stone chariot (a shrine carved to resemble a temple cart, dedicated to Garuda) and a hall of sculpted musical pillars — columns carved from single blocks of stone that produce distinct musical notes when tapped, though visitors are generally no longer permitted to strike them due to conservation concerns. This musical pillar design is considered a remarkable feat of both acoustic and architectural engineering for its time.

## Hampi — Krishnadevaraya

Krishnadevaraya, who ruled from 1509 to 1529, is widely regarded as the greatest ruler of the Vijayanagara Empire, presiding over its political, economic, and cultural peak. He was a noted patron of art, literature, and temple architecture (commissioning much of Vittala Temple's elaborate construction), and was himself an accomplished poet and scholar, credited with literary works in Telugu.

## Hampi — Hazara Rama Temple

The Hazara Rama Temple in the royal enclosure of Hampi is notable for its walls, which are extensively carved with relief panels depicting scenes from the Ramayana. These carvings served a didactic (teaching) function, visually narrating the epic to a largely non-literate general population who could not read Sanskrit or other literary texts directly.

## Hampi — Lotus Mahal and the Royal Enclosure

The Lotus Mahal, located within the Zenana (royal women's) enclosure, displays a distinctive blend of Hindu and Indo-Islamic architectural styles — combining traditional Hindu temple-style stepped elements with Islamic-style arches — reflecting cultural and architectural exchange between the Vijayanagara Empire (a Hindu kingdom) and neighboring Deccan Sultanates, despite the two often being political and military rivals.

## Hampi — Mahanavami Dibba

The Mahanavami Dibba is a large stepped stone platform within the royal enclosure of Hampi, used by Vijayanagara rulers to observe royal ceremonies, military parades, wrestling matches, and cultural performances — most notably during the Mahanavami festival (related to Navaratri/Dasara celebrations). Foreign travelers' accounts from the period describe elaborate festivities held here, involving displays of the empire's military strength and cultural richness.

## Hampi — The Fall of Vijayanagara (Battle of Talikota, 1565)

In 1565, a coalition of Deccan Sultanates (including Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, and Bidar) decisively defeated the Vijayanagara army at the Battle of Talikota (also known as the Battle of Rakkasa-Tangadi). Following the defeat, the city of Vijayanagara (Hampi) was captured, extensively looted, and largely destroyed over the following months. The empire itself did not immediately vanish — remnants of Vijayanagara rule continued for several more decades from other regional centers — but the capital never recovered, and Hampi was left in ruins, eventually being abandoned and largely forgotten until it was documented by British surveyors and historians in the 19th century.

## Sringeri — Overview

Sringeri, home to the Sringeri Sharada Peetham (one of four major monastic centers traditionally associated with the philosopher Adi Shankaracharya), holds particular significance to Vijayanagara history as the spiritual seat associated with Vidyaranya, the sage credited with guiding the empire's founding. The Vijayanagara rulers maintained a close relationship with this institution throughout much of the empire's history, reflecting the intertwining of religious authority and political legitimacy in the kingdom's self-image.

## Chitradurga Fort — Overview

Chitradurga Fort is a large hill fort in Karnataka, notable for its extensive rock-cut fortifications built into the natural granite hills of the region, constructed and expanded across several ruling periods including under later Vijayanagara-era chieftains (the Nayakas of Chitradurga).

## Chitradurga Fort — Onake Obavva

Onake Obavva is a celebrated folk heroine associated with Chitradurga Fort. According to widely told local legend, during a siege of the fort (traditionally dated to the 18th century, under the Nayaka rulers, and associated with an attack by Hyder Ali's forces), Obavva, the wife of a fort guard, discovered enemy soldiers attempting to infiltrate through a narrow rock crevice while her husband was away. Armed only with an "onake" (a wooden pestle used for pounding grain), she is said to have single-handedly killed several intruding soldiers one at a time as they attempted to squeeze through the narrow passage, preventing the fort from being overrun until reinforcements arrived. She remains a widely celebrated folk hero in Karnataka, particularly as an example of ordinary individual courage distinct from stories centered on kings and nobility.

## Karnataka Traditional Games and Crafts (Relevant to Vijayanagara-era Culture)

**Chowka Bara** is a traditional Karnataka board game played on a cross- or grid-shaped board, in the same broad family of games as Chaupar/Pachisi, historically played across South India using cowrie shells to determine moves.

**Channapatna toys** are a traditional Karnataka wooden toy craft, originating in the town of Channapatna (sometimes nicknamed "Gombegala Ooru," or "Toy Town" in Kannada), historically associated with lacquered, brightly colored, hand-turned wooden toys and figures. The craft's origins are often traced back to patronage during the Mysore Kingdom era, with a heritage reaching into earlier South Indian craft traditions.

**Pallanguzhi** is a traditional South Indian mancala-style board game played using a wooden board with rows of shallow pits, in which players distribute and capture seeds or shells according to set rules — historically popular across Tamil Nadu and Karnataka households.

**Yakshagana** is a traditional Karnataka dance-drama form combining music, dance, dialogue, elaborate costumes, and makeup, traditionally used to perform stories drawn from Hindu epics and mythology, particularly popular in the coastal and Malnad regions of Karnataka.
